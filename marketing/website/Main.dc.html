NEW YORK · 1984–1988

Every great room ran this setup.

In the mid-80s, the independent studios of New York ran this configuration day and night. Rooms like D&D and Power Play, where a generation of hip-hop records was cut. Drum machines printed straight to two-inch. Breaks flown in from vinyl. Vocals punched on the autolocator, one line at a time. Mixes done by hand, every fader up, every move live.

That sound came from the gear and from the way you had to work it. Commit to tape. Ride the faders. Let the machine push back.

If you worked in those rooms, you'll know your way around before the coffee's done. If you didn't, this is as close as it gets.

THE RIG

Nothing in a menu. Everything under your hands.

CMI HJ-24 tape machine

Twenty-four tracks on two-inch tape at 30, 15 or 7½ IPS. The reels have real weight: a full reel spools up slow and brakes soft, the lifters swing out on a wind, the pinch roller thunks in on play, and the reel motors whine up and down with the tape speed. Wind fast enough and the reels blur, the way they do in the room.

The capstan is quartz-locked: play is dead on speed, vari-speed ±20% when you want it. Reverse play for the backwards cymbal. A SMPTE wall clock over the autolocator reads the code off track 24.

AutoLocator III

The remote your hands already know. Type a time, hit LOC, and the machine winds there and lands on the cue. Ten memories, REP to loop between two of them, TVI for tape speed and pitch, record-ready and input keys on all 24 tracks.

Space to play. Comma and period to wind. Z to return to zero. R to punch in.

CMI HJ-632 inline console

The inline board, modeled from the original schematics strip for strip. Three-band EQ with sweepable mid and filters, six sends, 24 bus keys on every channel and long-throw faders at full size.

VCA groups, and fader automation the way it worked then: Off, Read, Write, Touch, Latch and Trim.

The rack and the patchbay

A Leximan 224X with its remote on the console. Peak limiters, multi-effects and digital delays from the era, plus your own AU and VST3 plugins racked beside them.

Patch it all with real cables. Drag from an output to an input and you're in.

The electronics, card by card

Open the drawers. Every channel has its REPRO, OUTPUT, RECORD / CUE and BIAS cards, trimpots and all, with its meter right above. Line up with the alignment tape and a screwdriver, or press ALIGN and let the machine do it.

Underneath, the real signal path: record and repro EQ for each speed, bias, the gap between the record and repro heads, punch-ins that land clean, sync playback, and tape that saturates when you push it.

TAPE

Pick your tape. Hear the difference.

Eight formulations on the shelf, each on its own reel, from a 10½-inch Scotia 250 to a 14-inch reel of Quantica GP9 holding 5,000 feet. Each one has its own level, headroom, hiss and appetite for bias. Thread one and the machine realigns for it, or keep your old alignment and hear the mismatch. Every reel keeps its own recordings, time code and place.

TWO ROOMS

Book room A or room B.

One button switches rooms. CMI: the HJ-24, the AutoLocator III and the HJ-632 inline desk. SLS: a 32-channel G-series-style console with its Studio Computer, and a Stauder A827 with its amber meter bridge and remote. The outboard rack stays with you.

SYNC

Your drum machine, locked to tape.

The Rowan SBX-80 sync box reads SMPTE off track 24 and turns it into a rock-steady MIDI clock with song position, so your TR, MPC or modern rig plays along with the tape. Stripe a new reel, tap in a tempo map, or load the transfer of an old two-inch and lock to the code that's already on it.

The SMPTE wall clock over the autolocator reads the same code off track 24, the way the clock on the studio wall used to.

No plugin windows. No mouse-hunting. Just the room.

Drawn for a 60-inch 4K screen, so every knob, key and meter is full size. Sit back from the desk and work the way you used to.
